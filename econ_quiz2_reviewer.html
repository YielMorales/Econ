Chapter 2 — How to Think and Do Macroeconomics

Method ▾

Thinking in a Macroeconomic Way

The single most important idea in the chapter is the fallacy of composition: what is true for one individual acting alone is not necessarily true when everyone does it. One person standing at a concert sees better; if everyone stands, nobody does. Macroeconomics therefore has emergent properties — it is not microeconomics scaled up, and macro conclusions cannot be obtained by simply adding together the behaviour of individual agents.

The mechanism that makes this bite is that one agent's spending is another agent's income. That is why the paradox of thrift works: if all households try to save more at once, aggregate spending falls, national income falls, and total saving can end up lower rather than higher.

The same error is committed when the government's finances are described as a household budget. A household must obtain income before it spends. A currency-issuing government is the source of the currency, so transferring the household logic to it is a fallacy of composition dressed up as common sense.

What Should a Macroeconomic Theory Explain?

The authors judge a theory by whether it can explain what is actually observed, not by its internal elegance or the sophistication of its mathematics. Six benchmarks are used:

  • Real GDP growth — persistent output gaps and slow recoveries.
  • Unemployment — mass unemployment that persists rather than clearing.
  • Real wages and productivity — since the 1980s real wages have grown more slowly than productivity, so the wage share of national income has fallen.
  • Private sector indebtedness — the gap between wages and productivity was filled by household borrowing, which built up fragility.
  • Central bank balance sheets — the enormous expansion of reserves after 2008 did not produce the accelerating inflation the quantity theory predicted.
  • Japan — the "glaring counterfactual": decades of very large fiscal deficits and the highest public debt ratio in the world, yet near-zero interest rates, no inflation crisis and no default.

Why Economists Disagree — The Minimum Wage

Economics cannot run controlled laboratory experiments. Evidence is always interpreted through a prior theoretical framework, and those frameworks carry value judgements. A competitive labour market model predicts that a minimum wage rise destroys jobs; other frameworks predict it need not. The same data are then read differently, so disagreement survives the evidence. This is also why the authors insist macroeconomics cannot be value-free — the choice of question, assumption and policy target is itself a value judgement.

The Structure of Scientific Revolutions (Kuhn)

  • Paradigm — the shared framework of assumptions, methods and accepted questions.
  • Normal science — puzzle-solving inside the paradigm; the fundamental assumptions are not themselves tested.
  • Anomalies → crisis → revolution — anomalies accumulate, confidence collapses, and a rival paradigm displaces the old one. Progress is not smooth accumulation.

Chapter 4 — National Income & Product Accounts

Measurement ▾

What GDP Is

GDP is the value of all final goods and services, evaluated at market prices, produced within a country in a given period. Intermediate goods are excluded to avoid double counting. GDP is a flow over a period — it is not a measure of a nation's wealth, which is a stock.

The Three Equivalent Measures

  • Expenditure (GDP): C + I (gross capital formation) + G + (X − M).
  • Production (GVA): gross output minus intermediate inputs, summed over all sectors — agriculture, industry, services.
  • Income (GDI): compensation of employees + consumption of fixed capital + taxes on production and imports − subsidies + net operating surplus.
Take one final good sold for ₱100. Its ₱100 can be read three ways: as the value added created in producing it, as the wages, profits and taxes generated in producing it, and as the ₱100 spent by its final buyer. Production = Income = Expenditure, by construction.

What Counts and What Doesn't

  • A car bought by a household is consumption; the same car bought by a firm is investment.
  • A new home enters as residential investment in the year completed, at the value of the construction only.
  • A pre-owned home is not counted — it was counted when built. Only the real estate agent's commission enters this year's GDP.
  • Imputed rent: owner-occupiers are treated as landlords renting to themselves, so the housing service is counted even with no market transaction.
  • Excluded: home cooking and unpaid household work, the black market, most of the informal sector, sales of used goods, purchases of financial assets (that is saving, not spending), intermediate goods, and government transfer payments (no production in exchange).

GDP vs GNI

GDP measures production located inside the borders. GNI measures income accruing to residents wherever earned: GDP plus net foreign income. For the Philippines, GNI exceeds GDP because of large net inflows from overseas Filipino workers. GNI per capita of about USD 4,850 (1 July 2026) put the country above the upper-middle-income threshold of USD 4,636.

GDP Is Not a Measure of Well-being

Pollution and social costs are not deducted, yet the clean-up spending and the medical treatment of those made sick by it are both added. Military equipment counts. Eliminating tobacco would raise well-being and lower GDP.

Real GDP, Deflators and Growth Rates

Nominal values are deflated to remove the price effect so that changes reflect the volume of output. Because relative prices drift, a fixed base year becomes less representative over time, which is why chain weighting updates the weights each period. Year-on-year comparison is preferred to quarter-on-quarter when a series is seasonal — a resort's Q1 can be far below Q4 and still be growing.

Chapter 6.1–6.3 — Sectoral Accounting

Identities ▾

Deriving the Sectoral Balances

Start from the income identity and add net foreign income:

GNI ≡ C + I + G + (X − M) + NFI

Subtract taxes net of transfers (T) from both sides:

(GNI − C − T) − I ≡ (G − T) + (X − M + NFI)

Which rearranges to the identity you must know cold:

(S − I) + (T − G) − CAB ≡ 0
  • (S − I) — private domestic financial balance.
  • (T − G) — government financial balance.
  • CAB = X − M + NFI — current account balance.

This is an accounting identity, true by construction. It tells you what must add up; it is not a behavioural theory of what causes what.

What the Identity Implies

  • For one sector to run a surplus, at least one other must run a deficit. All three cannot normally be in surplus.
  • The exception — the Golden Triangle — requires a current account surplus larger than the fiscal surplus. Rare; Sweden is the usual example.
  • In a country with a current account deficit, the private domestic sector can only save overall if the government runs a fiscal deficit larger than that current account deficit.
  • A government deficit equals, peso for peso, a non-government surplus.

Saving vs Savings

Saving (no "s") is a flow, a residual of income (S = Y − C − T), and involves changes in net financial assets. Savings (with an "s") is a gross stock of assets held — deposits, stocks, bonds, property. More savings does not imply more net worth. A rise in Saving does not automatically mean a rise in savings.

Net Financial Assets, Inside and Outside Wealth

  • Every financial asset is someone else's liability, so financial assets net to zero across sectors.
  • Inside wealth is claims held within the private sector — a bank loan raises one party's asset and another's liability, leaving the sector's net position unchanged.
  • Outside wealth comes from outside the private sector, i.e. claims on government. In a closed economy, net private financial wealth equals the public debt; with a permanently balanced budget it would be zero.
  • Real assets (buildings, machinery) are the exception — they are assets with no matching liability, so they do not net out.

Horizontal vs Vertical Transactions

  • Horizontal — within the non-government sector (bank loans, buying goods, buying shares). These do not change the sector's net financial assets.
  • Vertical — between government and non-government (spending, taxation). These do change the sector's net financial assets.

Causality

For an individual, income constrains spending. For the economy as a whole, the causation runs the other way: aggregate spending creates aggregate income. A society cannot decide to have more income, but it can decide to spend more.

Two Things the Chapter Corrects

  • The loanable funds story — save, deposit, lend, invest — is wrong. Banks create deposits when they lend; prior saving is not a precondition.
  • Reserve balances are liabilities of the central bank held by commercial banks. No private institution can create them; they are created when the central bank credits a bank's account.

Finally: while every point on the sectoral balances map is possible, the private domestic sector cannot run deficits indefinitely, because those deficits must be funded and accumulate as a rising stock of private debt.

Chapter 7.6 — Index Numbers

Technique ▾

What an Index Number Does

An index expresses each value of a variable as a percentage of a reference value — the base, set to 100. The unit of measurement disappears, which is exactly why you can put Korean and German tourist arrivals on the same chart and see which grew faster, even though one is twenty times larger than the other. Indices work for time series and for cross-sections (prices across provinces).

Composite Indices — Two Wrong-ish Options

For a composite like seafood (shrimp, tuna, squid) you can index the average price, or average the individual indices. These give different numbers. Indexing the average implicitly weights each item by its price level; averaging the indices gives every item equal weight regardless of how much of it people actually buy. Neither uses quantity information — which is what the weighted formulas fix.

The Three Weighted Price Indices

  • Laspeyres — uses base-period quantities as weights.
  • Paasche — uses current-period quantities as weights.
  • Fisher — the geometric mean of the Laspeyres and Paasche indices.
Because Laspeyres freezes base-period quantities, it ignores that consumers substitute away from goods that have become relatively more expensive — so it tends to overstate the rise in the cost of living. Paasche, using current quantities, tends to understate it.

Applied: the GDP deflator is a Paasche-type index (current-period weights). The CPI is a Laspeyres-type index (base-period basket weights) — in the Philippines specifically a chained Laspeyres.

Deflation

Deflation converts a nominal series (valued at each period's prices) into a real or constant-price series. When the monetary values are not the product of a price and a quantity — salaries, for example — you must choose a price index that reflects the prices actually faced by the group whose income you are deflating.

Linking and Re-basing

  • Linking two series with different bases: use the overlap year to get a conversion factor. If the old series (1990 = 100) reads 807.7 in 2021 and the new series is 2021 = 100, the factor is 8.077 — multiply the new series by it to extend the old one, or divide the old by it to extend the new.
  • Re-basing: divide every value in the series by the value in the intended new base year and multiply by 100.

Chain Indices and the CPI

A chain index updates the comparison base every period and links successive movements together, instead of comparing everything to a distant fixed year. The PSA builds the CPI from a market basket derived from the Family Income and Expenditure Survey, with each item weighted by its share of average household spending. Core inflation excludes selected food and energy items.

Two warnings worth memorising: a lower inflation rate does not mean prices have fallen — it usually means prices are still rising, only more slowly, while the accumulated burden remains. And two households with different spending patterns experience different personal inflation rates even when facing the same CPI.
